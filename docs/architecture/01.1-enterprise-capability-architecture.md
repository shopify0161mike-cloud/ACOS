# 1.1 Enterprise Capability Architecture

**System:** ACOS — Autonomous Commerce Operating System  
**Status:** Canonical  
**Authority:** Derived from the ACOS Constitution and Chapter 1.0 Enterprise Context Map  
**Purpose:** Define the complete enterprise capability model that governs how ACOS is decomposed, owned, prioritized, implemented, measured and evolved.

---

## 1.1.1 Purpose

The Enterprise Capability Architecture defines what ACOS must be able to do, independent of vendors, interfaces, workflows, prompts or individual technical implementations.

Capabilities are the stable bridge between strategy and execution. They translate constitutional intent into a governed implementation model that can be mapped to domains, services, workflows, data, APIs, events, AI agents, policies, tests and operating metrics.

This chapter is binding for all later architecture and implementation work.

---

## 1.1.2 Core rule

ACOS is designed around business capabilities, not around applications, screens, tools, vendors or workflow diagrams.

Technology is replaceable. Capabilities and business authority are not.

Every production component must trace to at least one approved capability. No component may be introduced solely because a tool supports it or because it appears operationally convenient.

---

## 1.1.3 Capability hierarchy

ACOS uses the following hierarchy:

```text
Enterprise
└── Domain Group
    └── Business Domain
        └── Capability
            └── Sub-capability
                └── Feature
                    └── Implementation
```

The hierarchy has these meanings:

- **Enterprise** — ACOS as one autonomous commerce operating system.
- **Domain Group** — Commerce, Intelligence, Governance or Platform.
- **Business Domain** — a bounded area of ownership, language, rules and data.
- **Capability** — a stable enterprise ability required to create value or protect the enterprise.
- **Sub-capability** — a more specific ability that contributes to a parent capability.
- **Feature** — user-visible or system-visible behavior that realizes part of a capability.
- **Implementation** — source code, schema, workflow, API, model, adapter, job, policy or infrastructure component.

Capabilities describe **what ACOS must be able to do**. Implementations describe **how ACOS currently does it**.

---

## 1.1.4 Capability design principles

### Stable over time

Capabilities should remain valid even when providers, models, frameworks or channels change.

### Business outcome oriented

Every capability must contribute to at least one measurable enterprise outcome such as revenue, margin, customer value, brand equity, operational efficiency, resilience, compliance or strategic learning.

### Single accountability

Every capability has one accountable business owner and one accountable technical owner. Contributors may be many, but accountability may not be ambiguous.

### Explicit boundaries

Each capability must define what it owns, what it consumes, what it produces and what it explicitly does not own.

### Traceable implementation

Every service, workflow, policy, database object, API, event and AI behavior must reference the capability or capabilities it implements.

### Governed autonomy

Capabilities that permit autonomous action must declare authority level, decision limits, confidence thresholds, approval requirements, reversibility and audit requirements.

### Measurable maturity

Each capability must have an explicit maturity level, operational state and measurable performance criteria.

## 1.1.6 Capability lifecycle

Capabilities move through the following states:

1. **Proposed** — identified but not yet approved.
2. **Approved** — accepted into the enterprise architecture.
3. **Planned** — scheduled and dependency-mapped.
4. **Implementing** — actively being built.
5. **Operational** — available in production and monitored.
6. **Improving** — operational but under active optimization.
7. **Deprecated** — retained temporarily but no longer strategic.
8. **Retired** — no longer permitted for use.

State transitions must be recorded and attributable.

A capability may not be marked Operational unless:

- its owner is assigned;
- its policies are defined;
- its interfaces are versioned;
- its acceptance criteria pass;
- its audit requirements are met;
- its operational metrics exist;
- its failure behavior is documented;
- its security controls are implemented.

---

## 1.1.7 Maturity model

ACOS uses five capability maturity levels:

### M0 — Undefined

No approved process, ownership or implementation exists.

### M1 — Defined

Purpose, ownership, interfaces and policies are documented.

### M2 — Implemented

The capability works in a controlled environment and satisfies functional acceptance criteria.

### M3 — Operational

The capability runs reliably in production with monitoring, audit, failure handling and security controls.

### M4 — Autonomous

The capability can make and execute authorized decisions within policy, risk and confidence boundaries.

### M5 — Adaptive

The capability continuously improves using outcome data without bypassing governance or altering its own authority.

A capability may not claim M4 unless all relevant actions are policy-evaluated, explainable, auditable and reversible where practical.

---

## 1.1.8 Criticality classes

Capabilities are assigned one of four criticality classes:

- **C1 — Supporting:** temporary failure has limited impact.
- **C2 — Important:** failure materially degrades operations but does not stop the enterprise.
- **C3 — Critical:** failure blocks a major commerce flow or governance control.
- **C4 — Constitutional:** failure compromises human authority, security, financial integrity, auditability or constitutional compliance.

C3 and C4 capabilities require explicit continuity, retry, recovery, escalation and incident-response behavior.

---

## 1.1.9 Authority classification

Every capability is assigned a maximum authority level:

- `observe`
- `analyze`
- `recommend`
- `execute_within_authority`
- `coordinate_autonomy`
- `approval_required`
- `human_exclusive`

The capability's maximum authority does not grant authority by itself. Runtime execution still requires applicable policy, confidence, budget, risk and identity checks.

## 1.1.11 Phase-one minimum autonomous capability set

The first operational phase must provide end-to-end autonomous commerce execution with only explicit human product acceptance reserved.

The minimum set is:

- market opportunity discovery;
- product discovery and validation;
- supplier discovery and qualification;
- unit economics and margin modeling;
- product risk analysis;
- product recommendation;
- approval package generation;
- authenticated product acceptance, including voice where configured;
- brand and offer generation;
- product content creation;
- creative generation coordination;
- storefront publication;
- campaign planning and deployment;
- budget-constrained campaign optimization;
- order and fulfillment monitoring;
- customer-service coordination;
- financial and performance monitoring;
- continuous learning from outcomes;
- audit and constitutional validation across the full chain.

No phase-one implementation may be described as autonomous when it still requires routine manual intervention after product approval.

## 1.1.12 Capability dependency model

Dependencies must be directional and explicit.

Each dependency has:

- source capability;
- target capability;
- dependency type;
- reason;
- criticality;
- synchronous or asynchronous mode;
- failure behavior;
- fallback behavior.

Allowed dependency types:

- `requires`
- `consumes_data_from`
- `publishes_to`
- `governed_by`
- `approved_by`
- `observed_by`
- `implemented_by`

Circular dependencies between capabilities require architecture review. Circular runtime calls between domains are prohibited unless explicitly approved and protected by timeout and failure-isolation rules.

---

## 1.1.13 Capability-to-domain mapping rules

Each capability has one primary owning domain.

A capability may depend on other domains but may not share ownership across domains.

The owning domain controls:

- terminology;
- invariant rules;
- state transitions;
- authoritative data;
- public contracts;
- business decision logic.

Cross-domain coordination must use commands, queries or events. Direct mutation of another domain's private storage is prohibited.

---

## 1.1.14 Capability-to-implementation traceability

Every implementation artifact must declare one or more capability identifiers.

This applies to:

- database migrations;
- database tables and views;
- domain services;
- APIs;
- event schemas;
- n8n workflows and nodes;
- AI prompts and policies;
- model evaluations;
- integration adapters;
- scheduled jobs;
- dashboards;
- alerts;
- tests;
- deployment manifests.

An implementation artifact without a capability reference is architecturally unowned and may not be promoted to production.

## 1.1.15 Capability contracts

Each operational capability must expose a contract containing:

- purpose;
- owner;
- allowed commands;
- supported queries;
- emitted events;
- consumed events;
- input schemas;
- output schemas;
- policy checks;
- authority level;
- idempotency behavior;
- timeout behavior;
- retry behavior;
- failure codes;
- audit fields;
- versioning policy.

Capabilities may be implemented by one or more services, but their external business contract must remain stable and implementation-independent.

---

## 1.1.16 AI capability contract

Any capability implemented partly or fully by AI must additionally define:

- model purpose;
- model provider independence strategy;
- required evidence;
- supported context window assumptions;
- deterministic controls where required;
- confidence calculation method;
- refusal conditions;
- escalation conditions;
- hallucination controls;
- evaluation dataset;
- quality thresholds;
- latency and cost limits;
- fallback model or non-AI fallback;
- logging and retention rules.

Prompt text is implementation detail and may not serve as the capability definition.

## 1.1.17 Capability metrics

Each capability must define at least one metric in each applicable category:

- business outcome;
- quality;
- reliability;
- latency;
- cost;
- risk;
- autonomy;
- human intervention rate.

For autonomous capabilities, the following metrics are mandatory:

- decision confidence distribution;
- approval rate where applicable;
- override rate;
- execution success rate;
- rollback rate;
- policy rejection rate;
- material error rate;
- average human intervention count;
- commercial outcome attribution.

Metrics must be governed by the Analytics domain and linked to decision and execution records.

---

## 1.1.18 Capability security model

Capabilities must declare a security classification:

- `public`
- `internal`
- `confidential`
- `restricted`

Restricted capabilities include at minimum:

- constitutional modification;
- authority management;
- secret management;
- payment credentials;
- financial control;
- approval identity verification;
- production deployment;
- audit record administration.

Least privilege applies at command, query, event and data-access level.

---

## 1.1.19 Recovery objectives

C3 and C4 capabilities must define:

- Recovery Time Objective (RTO);
- Recovery Point Objective (RPO);
- degraded operating mode;
- manual fallback;
- escalation path;
- data reconstruction procedure.

Governance and audit capabilities must fail closed when failure could permit unauthorized execution.

Commercial optimization capabilities may fail open only when doing so cannot create material risk or irreversible action.

---

## 1.1.20 Change governance

A capability change requires architecture review when it changes:

- ownership;
- domain boundaries;
- authority level;
- lifecycle state;
- criticality;
- externally visible contract;
- policy obligations;
- data ownership;
- autonomy level;
- dependency direction.

A constitutional amendment is required when a change affects human strategic authority, constitutional hierarchy or reserved decision rights.

---

## 1.1.21 Machine-readable registry

The canonical machine-readable capability inventory is stored at:

```text
config/capabilities/registry.yaml
```

The registry must validate against:

```text
schemas/capability-registry.schema.json
```

Human-readable documentation and machine-readable records must remain consistent. CI must reject invalid capability records.

## 1.1.22 Architecture review checklist

A capability is architecture-ready only when all answers below are explicit:

- What business outcome does it create or protect?
- Which domain owns it?
- Who is accountable?
- What authority may it exercise?
- What data does it own?
- Which commands, queries and events define its contract?
- Which policies govern it?
- What risks can it create?
- How is it secured?
- How is it measured?
- How does it fail?
- How is it recovered?
- Which implementations realize it?
- Which acceptance tests prove it?

## 1.1.23 Acceptance criteria

Chapter 1.1 is implemented when:

- a canonical capability registry exists;
- every capability has a unique stable identifier;
- every capability maps to one primary domain;
- owners, criticality, maturity and authority are explicit;
- dependencies are directional and validated;
- phase-one autonomy capabilities are represented;
- implementation artifacts can reference capability IDs;
- schema validation can reject incomplete or invalid records;
- README and architecture indexes point to this chapter;
- later domain and technical chapters use this model without redefining it.

## 1.1.25 Risks and controls

### Risk: capability model becomes documentation-only

**Control:** machine-readable registry, schema validation and mandatory implementation references.

### Risk: capability explosion

**Control:** capabilities must represent stable enterprise abilities, not individual tasks, screens or workflow steps.

### Risk: ambiguous ownership

**Control:** one accountable business owner and one technical owner per capability.

### Risk: autonomous authority expands silently

**Control:** authority level is explicit, versioned and policy-enforced.

### Risk: vendor coupling

**Control:** capabilities remain provider-independent and external platforms are mapped through adapters.

### Risk: false maturity claims

**Control:** maturity gates require measurable evidence and operational controls.

---

## 1.1.26 Architectural decisions

### ADR-0007 — Capability Registry as Source of Truth

**Decision:** ACOS maintains one canonical, machine-readable enterprise capability registry.

**Reason:** Prevents capability drift, unowned implementation and architecture based on tools rather than business outcomes.

### ADR-0008 — Single Primary Domain Ownership

**Decision:** Every capability has exactly one primary owning domain.

**Reason:** Shared ownership creates ambiguity in authority, data, state and accountability.

### ADR-0009 — Capability IDs Required in Implementation

**Decision:** Production implementation artifacts must reference one or more approved capability IDs.

**Reason:** Enables traceability from Constitution to code and prevents uncontrolled technical growth.

### ADR-0010 — Phase-One Autonomy Definition

**Decision:** Phase one is considered autonomous only when the full commerce lifecycle operates without routine human intervention after explicit product acceptance.

**Reason:** Prevents partial automation from being misrepresented as autonomous operation.

---

## 1.1.27 Final rule

No ACOS component may be built, deployed or granted autonomous authority unless it implements an approved capability, belongs to an accountable domain, satisfies the applicable policy model and can be traced back to the Constitution.
